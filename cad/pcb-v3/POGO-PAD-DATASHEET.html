TouchID — pogo pad datasheet

Everything you need to check J4 and J11 against the real keyboard slot. These ten pads are the last unverified geometry on the board, and the only numbers here that came from a measurement rather than a datasheet.

Why this matters. Every other dimension on this board is traced to a manufacturer drawing. These ten are not — they were measured off the keyboard and carried forward from pcb-v2. If they are wrong the module never makes contact, and the board is scrap. Nothing in software can check them.

1. Datum — read this before measuring anything

Origin is the centre of the board, not a corner. The board is 19.30 × 19.30 mm square with 2.0 mm corner radii, so the origin sits 9.65 mm from each edge.

+X is right, +Y is the spacebar edge. Both pogo blocks are at negative Y, i.e. the edge away from the spacebar.

The pads are on the BOTTOM of the board, so the drawing is mirrored. The table below gives each pad in board coordinates (the KiCad numbers). The drawing shows the board as you see it looking at the contact face — which flips X left-to-right. The table has a separate column for that. Measuring against the wrong handedness is the easiest way to waste a board, so check the column heading before you trust a number.

Check this first: J4 and J11 do not share a Y datum. J4's rows are at −7.22 / −4.72 / −2.22; J11's are at −7.20 / −4.70. That is a 0.02 mm offset between two connectors that presumably mate with the same pogo block.

0.02 mm is far below anything you can measure with calipers and almost certainly harmless against a sprung pin. But it is suspicious: two connectors on one grid should share a datum exactly. It suggests one of the two was transcribed separately. If your measurement says both blocks sit on the same pitch, tell me and I will align them.

4. How to check it

  1. Measure the keyboard, not the board. The board is the thing that has to be right; the slot is the thing that is right. Measure the pogo pin positions in the slot and compare them to the table.
  2. Get the pitch first. Pin-centre to pin-centre should be 2.50 mm in both axes. Pitch is easier to measure accurately than absolute position, and an error here is fatal because it compounds across the block.
  3. Then get one anchor. Measure from a board edge to the centre of one pin, on both axes. Everything else follows from the pitch. J4 pad 1 should sit 2.45 mm from the left edge and 2.43 mm from the bottom edge, in board coordinates.
  4. Check the block separation. J4 pad 2 to J11 pad 1 is 8.15 mm in X. If the two blocks are one moulded part this is the number most likely to be wrong.
  5. Check the pin lands inside Ø2.2. A pogo tip that lands within about 0.6 mm of a pad centre still makes contact. That is the real tolerance you are working to — not microns.

5. Fab notes for these pads

Ask for ENIG, not HASL. These are sliding/pressing contacts with no solder on them. HASL leaves an uneven tin surface that oxidises and wears; ENIG gives a flat, hard, corrosion-resistant gold surface. It is the single cheapest thing you can do to make the contact reliable, and it costs a few dollars on a prototype order.

They are mask-opened bare copper by design — that is correct for a contact pad and is why the finish matters. Board is 1.2 mm thick, 1 oz copper, 4 layers.

Do not let anything else onto this face. No via may be placed over these pads — a through-hole via would exit through the contact surface. The build enforces this, and it is why the ground-plane stitching avoids the whole pogo area.
