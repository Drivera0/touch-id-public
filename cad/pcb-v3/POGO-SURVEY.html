Pogo Pad Survey

Ten contacts on the underside of the module meet sprung pins in the keyboard. Their positions came from a task brief, not from calipers — and unlike every other dimension on this board, no datasheet can settle them. The keyboard decides.

10 pads J4 6 · J11 4 all Ø2.20 pitch 2.500 layer B.Cu screw M2 Ø2.0

Measure the button module, not the pins in the keyboard. The pins are recessed, springy and awkward to get a caliper near. The module sitting in that slot today has flat gold pads on its underside in exactly the positions our board must copy — and it fits in your hand.

Use the button module, not the knob: your own pin tests found the knob has no J11 contacts at all, so only the button module carries the full set of ten.

Three things worth resolving while it is in your hand

J4 and J11 are not on the same Y grid

J4's rows sit at −7.220 / −4.720 / −2.220; J11's at −7.200 / −4.700. A consistent 0.020 offset between two blocks meeting the same rigid keyboard.

Small enough not to matter, and small enough to be a typo carried forward. Those are different situations and nobody knows which this is.

Five of J4's six contacts go nowhere

Only J4.5 has a net, and it is ground. The other five are copper wired to nothing — the knob module's encoder and switch lines.

If the keyboard drives anything useful on them, five landing pads sitting unconnected is a wasted opportunity. If it drives something at a different voltage, it is worse than wasted.

How far can a pin wander before it walks off?

Ø2.20 is what the board draws. What matters is the contact patch — the shiny burnish mark the spring leaves after months of seating.

That mark is the honest number: it shows where the pin actually lands on this keyboard. The gap between the mark and the pad edge is the entire position-error budget for everything else on this page.
