What this session decides. Session 3 measured a steady 3.530 V on J11-1/2/3 with the backlight asleep — but that was an unloaded reading, and a 10 MΩ leak reads the same as a stiff supply on a multimeter. The load tests were run awake. So we have loaded numbers awake, unloaded numbers asleep, and no overlap. This fills the gap.

Pass → a self-contained module: pop it in, nothing modified, nothing to charge. Fail → open the keyboard and solder to its battery.
Why harvesting can work at all. The keyboard turns the module LED off by opening the return (J11-4), not by dropping the anodes — confirmed by the no-continuity result. So with the backlight asleep, J11-1/2/3 sit at 3.530 V with no path through the LED. Current we draw returns through J4-5, the real chassis ground. It never enters the LED, so it lights nothing and dims nothing.
Three specific risks.
  1. Never bridge J11-1/2/3 to J11-4. That is a direct LED overdrive path. Everything here returns to J4-5.
  2. Never tie J11-1/2/3 directly together. Three separate driver outputs. The 100 Ω isolation resistors exist so a backlight wake mid-test cannot make them fight.
  3. Minimum 100 Ω, always. No direct shorts, not even briefly. The voltages are harmless to you — the risk is to the keyboard.

Resistors you need

Read the bands from the end where they are bunched up — the gold tolerance band goes on the right. Always confirm in Ω mode anyway: 100 Ω and 10 kΩ differ by one band colour and are easy to swap.

Know your breadboard first

There are four rails, not two. The +/− pair on the left and the pair on the right are completely separate — not joined inside the board. If the load resistor went to the left − rail and the J4-5 jumper went to the right − rail, nothing would be connected and every reading would be meaningless. All three diagrams below use the LEFT pair. Stay on it.

What is actually joined to what

  • Each rail strip is one connection down its whole length. The groups of five printed on the rails are cosmetic — spacing marks to help you count. Electrically it is one long bus.
  • But check for a mid-rail break. Many boards split each rail in half. Look at the printed red or blue line beside the rail: a gap in the middle means two separate halves, and you must either bridge them with a jumper or keep everything on one half.
  • In the main area the groups of five are real. Holes a–e of one row are one connection; f–j of that same row are a different one. The centre channel is a genuine electrical gap.
  • Neighbouring rows are never joined. Row 4 and row 5 are independent — which is exactly what lets a resistor bridge between them.
Sixty seconds that will save you an hour. Keyboard off and unplugged, meter on continuity (beep), nothing else connected:
  1. Two holes at opposite ends of the same rail → should beep. No beep means the rail is split in the middle.
  2. Left − rail to right − rail → should NOT beep.
  3. Holes a and e of the same row → should beep.
  4. Holes e and f of the same row → should NOT beep. That is the centre channel doing its job.
  5. Hole a of row 4 and hole a of row 5 → should NOT beep.

Multimeter settings, phase by phase

Never turn the dial to A or mA in this session. In current mode the meter is almost a piece of wire. Putting it across a live rail is a short circuit — through your meter, and through the keyboard's LED driver. Every measurement here is DC volts.

That is also why the whole procedure works by measuring voltage across a known resistor and computing the current as I = V ÷ R. You never have to break the circuit to insert the meter, and you can never accidentally short anything.

On range: if your meter is autoranging, leave it alone — it picks for you. If it has fixed ranges, choose the smallest one above 3.5 V, usually marked 4V, 6V or 20V. A 4 V range resolves 1 mV; a 200 V range resolves 100 mV and would hide the entire effect you are looking for.

Why the unloaded reading is trustworthy: in volts mode the meter has about 10 MΩ of internal resistance, so it draws roughly 0.35 microamps. That is close enough to nothing that contact quality cannot affect the number — which is exactly why an unloaded 3.530 V proves a voltage exists but says nothing about whether current is available behind it.

No 330 Ω? Series three 100 Ω resistors. That gives 300 Ω, which is fine — predicted readings become J11-1 = 1.994 V, J11-2 = 1.412 V, J11-3 = 1.352 V, and the combined node = 2.340 V instead of 2.414. Resistors in series simply add. Enter 300 in place of 330 when you read the verdict table.
Each resistor bridges from its row to the next, so the current runs through all three end to end. Row 4 is the pin; row 13 hops across to the − rail.

Wattage: ¼ W is fine everywhere with margin to spare. The worst case in this whole session is the 100 Ω load if the source turned out perfect, which would be 125 mW — half the rating. Every other combination is under 40 mW.

There are two different ways the backlight goes dark and they may not be the same electrical state. Measure both, no load connected, everything referenced to J4-5.

  • State A — sleep timeout. Leave the keyboard untouched until the backlight drops on its own.
  • State B — brightness zero. Set brightness to 0 manually while the keyboard is otherwise awake.

Setting it up

Nothing gets built for this phase. No breadboard, no resistors, no jumpers. Meter probes straight into the empty slot.

  1. Module removed from the slot, keyboard on. Record whether you are wired or wireless.
  2. Meter on DC volts, lowest range above 3.5 V.
  3. Clip the black lead to the USB-C metal shell. Session 1 confirmed J4-5 beeps to it, so it is the same node — and clipping to a shell is far easier than balancing a probe on a pogo pin while you work. No current flows in this phase, so contact quality cannot affect the reading either way.
  4. Touch the red probe to J11-1, then J11-2, J11-3, J11-4. One pin at a time, tip vertical, gentle — the risk here is bridging two pins with the probe tip, not the voltage.

Getting into each state: for State A, leave the keyboard completely alone until the backlight drops on its own, then measure without touching any keys — typing will wake it and you will have to start the wait again. For State B, set brightness to 0 in the keyboard software and measure promptly, before the sleep timer drifts you into State A.

Breadboard setup 1 — single pin

red probe → row 4, hole c black probe → − rail jumper wire use the left rail pair throughout RL = load, swap 10 kΩ / 1 kΩ / 330 Ω / 100 Ω

Held this way — letters across the top, numbers down the side — a node runs sideways. The five holes a to e in row 4 are all one connection, and f to j in row 4 are a separate one. That is how the incoming wire (hole e), the resistor (hole a) and the red probe (hole c) all join without touching each other.

The − rail is only ground because the J4-5 jumper makes it so — a breadboard has no inherent ground. Rows 4 and 10 are arbitrary; any two rows work as long as each component's legs land where the drawing says.

Procedure

  1. Ground first, always. Jumper the − rail to J4-5 before connecting anything to J11.
  2. Jumper 10e to the pin under test.
  3. Load out — record the unloaded voltage. No current flows, so contact quality cannot affect this number.
  4. 1 kΩ in, read within ~10 s. Then 330 Ω. Then 100 Ω.
  5. Re-seat your grip and repeat each reading 4–5 times. Keep the highest. Contact resistance can only pull a reading down, never up.
  6. Lift the J11 jumper first, then the ground jumper.
Why three load points instead of one. A genuine resistive source gives three readings that fall on a straight line. Leakage does not. One point can be faked by a bad contact — three cannot.

Phase 1b — the four-cell matrix

You measured wireless + asleep. That is one of four cells, and it may be the worst one.

A wireless keyboard runs on its own battery and power-saves aggressively. With the backlight off and no cable attached, the most likely thing the firmware does is shut the LED driver down entirely — leaving only the leakage path you measured. Plugged in, with mains power available, it has no reason to bother.

Session 3's 231 Ω came from an awake measurement whose wired/wireless state was never recorded. So the two numbers that disagree by 50× differ in two variables at once, and nobody has separated them.

What each outcome means
  • Wired · asleep comes back strong → the keyboard powers down its LED driver only on battery. Harvesting works whenever the cable is in. Not the self-contained module you wanted, but it would keep an on-board cell topped up for free with no keyboard modification.
  • Both awake rows strong, both asleep rows dead → harvest while you type, coast the rest of the time. The backlight is on exactly when you are at the keyboard, so this is a perfectly good fit — as long as no dimming is visible.
  • All four weak → harvesting is genuinely dead and the keyboard-battery tap is the answer. At that point the next test is tracing J4-2 and J4-6.

This is the phase that answers the question. Each pin gets its own 100 Ω isolation resistor; the three meet at a common node; one load resistor runs from that node to J4-5. Backlight off only — do not run this phase awake.

Breadboard setup 2 — combined node

R1 R2 R3 = 100 Ω isolation RL = load, swap 10 kΩ / 1 kΩ / 330 Ω / 100 Ω red probe → row 14, hole e black probe → − rail

Reading the layout. Each pin arrives in hole e of its own row — J11-1 at row 2, J11-2 at row 6, J11-3 at row 10 — so its wire runs out to the right across empty board. Its isolation resistor runs down its own lettered column to row 14, which becomes the common harvest node because all five of its holes are one connection.

Row 14 uses all five: a b c for the three resistors, d for the load resistor down to row 20, e for the red probe. From row 20 a short jumper hops left onto the − rail, which runs out to J4-5.

The isolation resistors are the safety feature. Without them, a backlight wake mid-test would tie three separately driven outputs together.

Procedure

  1. Build the node with no load resistor installed. Record the unloaded node voltage — should still be ~3.530 V.
  2. 1 kΩ from node to − rail. Record. Then 330 Ω. Then 100 Ω.
  3. Repeat the whole set in the other lights-off state (A vs B).

No ground wire on the breadboard for this phase — the current returns to ground inside the keyboard. The black probe only needs a reference.

If J11-4 is a switched sink, it can tell the module when to stop harvesting using one pin and one resistor instead of sampling PWM on three ADC channels. On a module living off a supercap, that saving is not cosmetic.

No external supply needed — borrow the 3.53 V already sitting on J11-1. Put 100 kΩ from J11-1 to J11-4, then measure J11-4 against ground in both states.

Yes, this deliberately bridges J11-1 to J11-4 — the thing I told you never to do. The difference is the resistor. That warning is about low resistance: a bare wire or a 100 Ω would dump current straight through the LED. 100 kΩ limits it to about 35 µA — roughly a thousandth of what it takes to light an LED visibly, and nothing any driver pin will notice. Do this with 100 kΩ and nothing smaller. If you see any glow at all, that itself is data — write it down.

Setting it up
